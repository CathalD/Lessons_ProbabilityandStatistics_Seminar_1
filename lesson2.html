0–15 minutes · Concept introduction

What connects the outcomes?

Can two experiments have identical individual distributions and different relationships?

A marginal distribution describes one variable by itself. A joint distribution records pairs. A conditional distribution describes a target after some information is known. Under independence, that information leaves its distribution unchanged. Dependence can arise from an earlier stage, a shared influence or a constraint. Correlation by itself does not identify the mechanism.

p(x,y)=pX(x)p(y|x)

In a discrete model, condition on an event with positive probability. For example, knowing the controller result changes which total distribution applies. Repeating a reset two-stage experiment can still give independent trials.

Read with this concept · Penn State · Covariance and independence

Independent contributions

Fresh A and fresh B. Observing A gives no information about B in this model.

A shared contribution

X = C + A and Y = C + B. The same rolled C appears in both totals.

A fixed total

A + B = 14. More in one cup means less in the other.

A short temporal example

Start with a fair die value. On controller faces 1–3 keep the previous value; on 4–6 draw a fresh value. The marginal distribution stays uniform, while neighbouring times are dependent. With stationary initialization, lag-h correlation is (1/2)ʰ. A reset experiment carries nothing between trials. Running and plotting this time sequence is an extension.

15–50 minutes · A two-stage experiment

Let the first roll choose the experiment

Does knowing the number of contributions improve a prediction of the total?

A controller chooses N. Its faces 1–2 mean one die, 3–4 mean two dice, and 5–6 mean three dice. Then roll N fresh independent dice and add them. An individual later die retains its fair face probabilities, but the total S depends on N. The ecological parallel is total seed output from a variable number of contributing plants.

S=∑j=1NDjE[S|N=n]=3.5n

Expected value is the model’s weighted average. Before seeing N, average over the three conditional distributions with weight 1/3 each. After seeing N, use its own distribution. The complete experiment resets between trials.

Worked tool: for two dice, totals 10, 11 and 12 have 3 + 2 + 1 ordered combinations. Before seeing N, the event probability is (0 + 1/6 + 5/8)/3 = 19/72.

Empirical tool: p̂(y | N = n) = count(N = n and Y = y) / count(N = n). Write the corresponding conditional CDF. A condition with no observations has no empirical estimate.

Read with this concept · MIT Tutorial 6, question 3 · A die-controlled repeated-trial experiment

Evaluation tool: Brier score = mean((q − o)²), where q is a forecast event probability and o is 1 if the event occurs and 0 otherwise. A forecast q = 0.7 followed by the event contributes (0.7 − 1)² = 0.09. Compare models on the same outcomes; lower is better on that set.

50–65 minutes · A tool for paired variation

Describe what changes together

When one cup is above its usual total, is the other above, below or unrelated to its usual total?

Covariance averages products of paired deviations from their means. Same-direction deviations contribute positively; opposite directions contribute negatively. Correlation divides covariance by both standard deviations, giving a dimensionless quantity when both spreads are nonzero. A near-zero sample correlation does not establish independence.

sXY=1n−1∑i=1n(Xi−X¯)(Yi−Y¯)

Here n is the number of pairs and the bars denote sample means. For theoretical means (7, 7), the pair (9, 8) contributes (9 − 7)(8 − 7) = 2. Its contribution is positive; it is not the complete covariance.

Compare two independent two-dice cups with X = C + A and Y = C + B, where C is rolled once. Both marginals stay the same two-dice distribution. Their true-total covariances are 0 and Var(C) = 35/12; the shared model has correlation 1/2.

75–105 minutes · Exchange and genetic mixing

What does a moved die carry?

Does exchanging objects change the next measurement, and which property persists?

Each cup contains two dice. A controller chooses zero, one or two dice to exchange in each direction, with equal probabilities. Reset before each replicate. First preserve the faces 1, 2, 5 and 6: the true cup totals always sum to 14. Then exchange identical fair dice and roll them all afresh: independent two-dice totals return.

For the migration analogy, die type persists while rolled outcomes change. Low faces are 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2; high faces are 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9. These profiles are disclosed in this controlled Lesson 2 model. Reset A to two low dice and B to two high dice, exchange k dice, and roll all dice afresh.

E[TA|k]=2+7kE[TB|k]=16−7k

Worked tool: when k = 1, each cup has one low type (mean 1) and one high type (mean 8), giving mean total 9. When k = 0, the means are 2 and 16. Plot by k and use paired observations to predict the other cup.

This models migration between populations. Reproduction, inheritance and recombination would need additional rules. The negative association in this example comes from a fixed shared pool; it is not a general claim that genetic similarity is negative.

Read with this concept · Carnegie Mellon · Bernoulli–Laplace exchange; Holsinger · Migration between populations

Extension · Continue the population history

Keep composition between time steps instead of resetting. Use a no-exchange option and a one-die exchange option. Retain the time order and analyse this history separately. Exchanging both dice merely swaps whole cup compositions; it does not guarantee within-cup mixing.

105–135 minutes · A spatial construction

Nearby sites share contributions

How does information change with the separation between locations?

Generate seven independent fair-die values for one experimental world. Five sites use overlapping groups of three. Record each Z once on a shared board; do not re-roll it when it contributes to another site. Every new world regenerates all seven values. All sites retain the same three-dice marginal distribution.

Site 1Z1 + Z2 + Z3
Site 2Z2 + Z3 + Z4
Site 3Z3 + Z4 + Z5
Site 4Z4 + Z5 + Z6
Site 5Z5 + Z6 + Z7

Theoretical tool: shared independent contributions add their variances to covariance. At separations 0, 1, 2 and 3, there are 3, 2, 1 and 0 shared draws. The corresponding true-total covariances are 8.75, 5.833, 2.917 and 0.

Empirical tool: estimate the covariance of fixed paired sites across independent worlds. Then compare pairs with the same separation. Five linked readings within a world are not five independent worlds.

Read with this concept · Rasmussen and Williams · Covariance functions; Shared random contributions

A field often provides one spatial realization. Under an appropriate stationary mean and residual covariance model, we can pool pairs at similar separation. Each pair contributes half its squared difference to an empirical semivariogram. A pair differing by 2 contributes 2.

γ(h)=12E[(Ti−Ti+h)2]=C(0)−C(h)

The last equality needs the stated stationary structure. Independent sensor error adds variance at each site; a nugget can also represent unresolved spatial variation. Shared observer error can create apparent covariance.

135–155 minutes · Guided field model

Use spatial structure to predict a measurement

Can nearby soil measurements improve a prediction at a held-back location?

A covariance function describes the relationship a model assigns to locations. A Gaussian process additionally assumes every finite collection has a joint Gaussian distribution. The dice remain discrete; they demonstrate the covariance idea rather than being a Gaussian process.

Use a working model for natural-log zinc with a constant mean and signal covariance C(d) = σ² exp(−d/ℓ). Length scale ℓ describes exponential decay; it is not a finite cutoff. A valid covariance matrix must be positive semidefinite. The supplied R helper handles the matrix calculation so you can inspect the model and its predictions.

Read with this concept · Rasmussen and Williams · Gaussian processes and conditional prediction; Meuse soil data

Compare a global-mean baseline with the spatial prediction on the same held-back measurements. RMSE = square root of mean squared error, on a declared scale. For observed log zinc 5.5 and prediction 5.8, squared error is 0.09. A log-Gaussian model’s exp(predicted log mean) is a median; its mean includes a variance correction.

The current split tests interpolation among sampled locations. A separate distant region asks a different question. Choose validation to match the intended use, and fit any parameters using training observations only. Predicting a laboratory measurement differs from predicting the latent true soil field.
