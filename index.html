Quick lesson · Repeated observations

From counts to probabilities

A frequency answers “How many times did this happen?” Divide by the number of trials to estimate how often it happens. This is our frequentist starting point: learn a fixed process from repeated trials, then test what the estimate predicts.

Write the rule

p^(x)=n(x)N

n(x) is the count at value x; N is the total number of observations. The hat marks an estimate.

Use it to solve a question

Estimate the chance of a value from 2 to 4 inclusive. Add the relative frequencies for 2, 3 and 4. For a known fair die the theoretical answer is 3/6; your estimate will vary.

An empirical frequency of zero means “not observed in this sample.” It does not establish impossibility.

Quick lesson · A distribution is a model

One die. Two dice. Three dice.

Adding independent dice changes the distribution of the total. Repeating an experiment gives more data about that distribution. These are two different operations.

The mass function

pS(s)=P(S=s)

Each bar is the probability of one possible total. Its height is probability, not a continuous density.

The cumulative function

FS(x)=P(S≤x)

Accumulate the mass at every total at or below x. The CDF is a step function.

P(a≤S≤b)=FS(b)−FS(a−1)

For integer-valued totals and integer endpoints.

Check the selected interval

Three regular dice have 6³ = 216 equally likely ordered face combinations, but only 16 possible totals: 3–18. Three dice are still discrete; a smooth normal curve would be an approximation.

Game 1 · Learn the method

The regular dice experiment

Use three ordinary dice. Observe through the fuzzy cup, build a model from your measurements, and make predictions for ten fresh opaque-cup rolls. Keep reference values out of your training notebook.

Game 2 · Discover a different process

New dice. New distribution.

Replace the dice with three six-sided dice whose faces have values between 0 and 9. Values may repeat and the dice may differ. Discover the process from rolls; the face lists are not shown.

Build a new model from new fuzzy observations. Choose intervals, predict ten new opaque rolls and compare the results. The method stays the same even when the outcome distribution changes.

Optional bridge · Continuous measurements

When bars become a density

Dice totals use a mass function p(x) and a cumulative function F(x). For a continuous measurement model, f(x) denotes a density. The probability of an interval is an area, not the height of the curve.

f(x)=1σ2πe−(x−μ)22σ2
P(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

Gaussian and normal name the same family. The standard normal has μ = 0 and σ² = 1. More raw observations do not make a process normal. The central limit theorem concerns standardized sums or means under assumptions such as independent, identically distributed values with finite variance.

Seminar discussion

What did the observations let you know?

The empirical distribution of fuzzy readings estimates pY, the distribution of observations. Using it to predict true totals assumes the measurement error is small or has been accounted for. The validation experiment can expose a mismatch; it does not automatically identify its cause.

Lesson 2 will change the independence assumptions: linked outcomes, swapping dice between cups, covariance and Gaussian processes.

Notation and references

Use P(event) for a probability; p(x) for discrete mass; F(x) for a CDF; and f(x) for continuous density. A hat indicates an estimate. Some books use f for a discrete mass function too; the distinction is the type of variable, not the letter alone.
