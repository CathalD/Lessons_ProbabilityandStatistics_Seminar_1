Lesson 2 overview and timing

Conditional prediction and linked observations • Three hours

The scientific question is whether information about one outcome improves a prediction for another. We will distinguish dependence within a two-stage trial, dependence between paired measurements, and dependence carried across time or space. Students must retain the links between observations when constructing a model.

Learning outcomes

  • Define marginal, joint and conditional probabilities and estimate conditional event frequencies.
  • Use a known mechanism to derive a benchmark, compare it with observations and evaluate a conditional forecast against an unconditional baseline.
  • Interpret positive, negative and near-zero sample covariance without equating correlation with a causal mechanism or independence.
  • Explain cup exchange as a controlled migration analogy and shared contributions as a spatial dependence model.
  • Read a covariance function and a spatial prediction with uncertainty, and explain why the validation design must match the prediction target.

Lesson 2 stage A Recognising dependence

0–15 minutes • A scientific question and a short comparison

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: Penn State on covariance and independence [R4]. Use a diagram of two independently generated totals beside a diagram with one shared contribution. The opening visual preserves each marginal distribution so that the relationship, rather than a changed histogram, is the focus.

Opening explanation

A marginal distribution describes one variable on its own. A joint distribution records paired outcomes. A conditional distribution describes the target after some information is known. Independence means that this information does not change the target distribution. Dependence can come from an earlier stage, a shared driver or a constraint; a correlation alone does not identify which mechanism produced the data.

Define the conditioning event and require it to have positive probability in the discrete example. Ask students whether two separate histograms can establish independence. The answer is no: pairing is necessary to see the relationship.

Experiment and comparison

Minutes 0–4: establish the question and notation. Minutes 4–9: half the groups produce five independent two-dice pairs; the other half use the shared-draw rule introduced in stage C. Minutes 9–12: pool the pairs into two scatterplots, with identical axes, and compare the observed patterns with the mechanism diagrams. Five pairs are an initial illustration, not an independence test.

Minutes 12–15: commit to an initial prediction for an opaque partner after a high first total, then compare the reasoning across groups. Explain that the detailed probability tools follow in stage B and covariance follows in stage C. Retain the initial claim so students can revise it later.

Show what crosses a trial boundary

Use a short pre-generated sequence to contrast resetting a game with carrying a value forward. A keep-or-refresh rule retains the previous fair-die value with probability one half and otherwise redraws it independently. Its marginal distribution stays uniform while adjacent observations are dependent. Students annotate which information persists. Running a long sequence and estimating autocorrelation are optional follow-up tasks.

Required record: trial identifier, both paired observations, mechanism, source of the data and any observer identifier. Do not split or independently sort paired columns. Clear reference values remain separate from the learner training data.

Lesson 2 stage B Random sums

15–50 minutes • Theory then observation then conditional prediction

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: MIT Tutorial 6 question 3 [R3], a close die-controlled repeated-trial example. Our dice-total variation is a course design. For the core physical task, map controller faces 1–2 to N = 1, 3–4 to N = 2 and 5–6 to N = 3. This keeps three conditioning groups. The original N = 1–6 version remains an extension.

The ecological parallel is aggregate seed output: the number of contributing plants affects a total even when each plant follows the same contribution model. The dice provide a known mechanism for learning the tools before considering biological assumptions.

Opening explanation and tools

The controller chooses the number of independent contributions. A large N does not change the face probabilities of an individual later die, but it changes the distribution of the total. A probability before seeing N averages over the possible conditions; a probability after seeing N uses the appropriate conditional distribution. The experiment resets completely between trials.

Lesson 2 stage C Measuring paired variation

50–65 minutes • Covariance as a tool for describing the relationship

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: covariance and correlation definitions [R4] and the shared-dice construction [R7]. Begin with the practical question: if one cup is above its usual total, is the other usually above, below or unrelated to its usual total?

Opening explanation and worked calculation

Covariance averages the products of paired deviations from their means. Same-direction deviations contribute positively and opposite-direction deviations contribute negatively. Correlation rescales covariance by the two standard deviations. It is dimensionless and requires both standard deviations to be nonzero. A small sample covariance is not evidence that two variables are independent.

Work the pair (9, 8) against theoretical means (7, 7): its product contribution is 2. On a scatterplot, shade the two positive and two negative quadrants. Distinguish this single contribution from a complete sample covariance.

Theoretical comparison

For independent two-dice cup totals the population covariance is zero. For a single shared fair-die value C plus independent local dice A and B, define X = C + A and Y = C + B. Roll C only once and record it beside both cups. Re-rolling the same physical die generates a new value and removes that shared contribution.

Both designs retain the same individual two-dice distributions. This isolates what pairing adds to a model. A positive association in observational field data could also reflect a shared driver, rather than mutual influence.

Experiment and prediction

Spend 5 minutes on concepts and the worked contribution, 4 on pooled pairs from stage A plus additional simulated samples, 3 on student scatterplots and covariance, and 3 on an opaque partner prediction. Compare the empirical covariance with 0 or 35/12 as appropriate, allowing for sampling and measurement effects.

A reference prediction is P(Y ≥ 9) = 5/18 before observing X, while P(Y ≥ 9 | X = 10) = 1/2 in the shared model. Ask students to explain the change before checking the exact enumeration. For undefined empirical conditions, show the missing sample size and avoid inventing an estimated probability.

Lesson 2 stage D Exchange and genetic mixing

75–105 minutes • A controlled migration analogy

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: the Bernoulli–Laplace exchange model [R5] and Holsinger on migration between populations [R6]. Open with the question: what does an exchanged object carry that can change the next observation? Each cup contains two dice throughout; exchange the same number in both directions.

Opening explanation

An exchange can preserve a rolled value, change the number or type of contributions, or change nothing relevant after a fresh roll. The model must state which property persists. Moving persistent types between populations provides a migration analogy. The activity has no reproduction, segregation or recombination mechanism, so it does not model those genetic processes.

Main experiment

Use low faces 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2 and high faces 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9. These reference profiles are disclosed for this controlled Lesson 2 model. Reset A to two low types and B to two high types before each independent replicate. A controller chooses k = 0, 1 or 2 with equal probability. Exchange k dice in each direction, roll all dice afresh and record the paired totals. The count k determines the number of high types in A.

The exact covariance for this reset protocol is −98/3 and its correlation is −49/51. The negative association comes from the fixed shared pool of types. It is not a general prediction about genetic covariance in neighbouring populations. These values are instructor checks and optional derivations.

Allocate 6 minutes to the explanation and fixed-value example, 4 to the identical-dice control, 10 to approximately 15 physical paired trials per group, 5 to pooled plots and conditional estimates, and 5 to a locked partner forecast and fresh checks. Simulation supplies a ten-pair validation set when physical collection is slow. All learners complete one prediction and comparison.

Students draw the mechanism, plot the three k groups and compare with the conditional means. Discuss why a high total in A can inform a prediction for B even when the later dice are independent given composition. A separate continuing-history mode uses a no-exchange option and a one-die exchange option; preserve its time order and analyse it separately from reset trials. Swapping both dice simply exchanges the two cup compositions and does not guarantee within-cup mixing.

Lesson 2 stage E Shared contributions across space

105–135 minutes • Equal marginal distributions and changing dependence

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: shared random variables [R7] and covariance functions [R9]. Begin with the field question: how much does a nearby measurement tell us about an unmeasured location? Nearby locations can share environmental contributions, while more distant locations share fewer.

Theoretical construction

For each independent experimental world, generate seven independent fair-die values Z1 to Z7. Five sites use overlapping groups of three. Use labelled cards or a shared board to represent a rolled value in several totals; it is the same draw each time it appears. Every new world regenerates all seven contributions.

All five marginals remain three-dice distributions. Derive the covariance by adding the variance of the shared contributions. The valid covariance matrix comes from this explicit construction; assigning arbitrary pairwise correlations need not produce a valid joint model.

Empirical work and prediction

Use 6 minutes for theory, 8 for five to ten physical worlds per group and pooled observations, 7 for plots and covariance by separation, 6 for ten fresh simulated validation worlds, and 3 for interpretation. Aim for at least 30 pooled training worlds. The independent replicate is a world; its five site values are linked.

Students draw the overlapping contributions, calculate one covariance from paired sites across worlds, and sketch covariance versus separation. They then lock a marginal interval forecast and a neighbour-conditioned forecast for an opaque site, using all training observations but no validation outcomes. A few conditional observations support only a coarse empirical model; display the sample size.

A bridge to field data

An actual field often supplies one spatial realization. Under an appropriate common-mean, stationary residual model, pairs at similar separations can inform an empirical semivariogram. Introduce half the squared difference of a pair as a visual measure of dissimilarity; a worked pair differing by 2 has a contribution of 2. [R13]

The equality assumes the relevant stationary mean and covariance structure. Independent additive measurement error increases the variance at each site and the semivariogram at nonzero separation. A nugget can also reflect unresolved spatial variation; it cannot automatically be identified as instrument error. Shared observer errors can create apparent spatial covariance.

Lesson 2 stage F A spatial prediction model

135–155 minutes • Guided Gaussian process and R transfer

Use the corresponding student introduction for equations and visual examples.

Read first: Rasmussen and Williams on Gaussian processes [R8], Meuse data documentation [R12], and the prediction-target discussion in Roberts and colleagues [R14]. The field question is to predict a zinc measurement at a sampled location whose outcome is held back.

Opening explanation

A covariance function describes the relationship a model assigns to pairs of locations. A Gaussian process additionally assumes that every finite collection has a joint Gaussian distribution. Shared dice demonstrate a covariance pattern but remain discrete. A spatial model can borrow information from nearby measurements and quantify remaining predictive uncertainty under its assumptions.

For a short guided example, model log zinc with a constant mean and an exponential covariance. The instructor supplies a helper that converts training data and new coordinates into predictions. Students define the target, inspect the map, compare a global-mean baseline with the spatial result, and interpret the interval. Deriving the matrix calculation is optional.

The length scale ℓ is the distance over which covariance falls by a factor of e in this model; it is not a finite cutoff. The companion worked example fixes ℓ at 500 m and a nugget fraction at 0.10 as declared teaching choices. Mean and variance are estimated using training observations only. These choices are not facts supplied by the Meuse study.

Practical sequence

Use 5 minutes for the concept and covariance curve, 5 to inspect a map of the 140 training sites and empirical semivariogram output, 6 to run or read the supplied R helper and freeze predictions for 15 hidden sites, and 4 to discuss uncertainty and the validation design. Student observations support a fitted model; there is no known true soil distribution available for comparison.

The selected 15 holdouts lie among training sites. That asks an interpolation question. Predicting a separate northern region asks a different question; show it as a follow-up comparison rather than asserting that one splitting scheme answers every task. If estimating a variogram or tuning parameters in cross-validation, redo that estimation inside each training fold. [R14]

Predict held-back measurements, not an unobserved true soil field. Use natural-log units for RMSE. Under the working log-Gaussian model, exp(predicted log mean) is a predictive median; a predictive mean includes the variance correction. Intervals in the worked script condition on fitted parameters and omit their estimation uncertainty. Students state this limitation before seeing the held-back values.

Forestry answers

The conditional probabilities are 0/10 = 0 for diameter at most 12 inches and 8/11 ≈ 0.727 for larger diameters. The overall training probability is 8/21 ≈ 0.381. An empirical zero expresses what was observed in ten small training trees; it is not a biological impossibility claim.

With five test trees in each group, the conditional expected count is 5 × 0 + 5 × 8/11 = 40/11 ≈ 3.64. The overall baseline predicts 80/21 ≈ 3.81. Similar overall expected counts can conceal different predictions for individual trees. The held-back observations contain four high-volume trees, all in the larger-diameter group. The worked conditional Brier score is approximately 0.0826.

Award credit for a limited, explicit prediction population and the recognition that a coarse diameter split does not account for every source of volume variation. The data demonstrate association, and the exercise does not establish a causal effect by itself. Code selects test rows 3, 6, …, 30 and estimates probabilities only on the remaining 21 trees.

Soil answers and computed reference

There are 140 training sites and 15 fixed interpolation holdouts, selected as dataset rows 10, 20, …, 150. The worked constant-mean, exponential-kernel model has baseline RMSE 0.7807 and spatial RMSE 0.3782 in natural-log zinc units. All 15 measurements fall within the working 95 percent intervals in this particular split. This is a limited diagnostic, and does not establish 95 percent calibration or universal superiority of the spatial model.

Extensions and preparation

Extensions after the core seminar

  • Use the original controller N = 1–6. The mean total is 49/4 = 12.25 and its variance is 735/16 = 45.9375. Compare with the core N = 1–3 mean of 7 and variance of 14.
  • Study the average M = S/N. Its conditional mean remains 3.5 while its conditional variance is 35/(12N). This provides dependent variables with zero covariance and a visual introduction to changing variance.
  • Run keep-or-refresh time sequences. With a one-half retention probability and a stationary initial fair draw, the lag-h correlation is (1/2)^h. A zero-retention control provides independent observations.
  • Run continuing migration histories and compare them with reset replicates. Track composition as the state; noisy totals alone need not identify that state.
  • Fit a semivariogram to field residuals using gstat, inspect trend and anisotropy, and compare candidate covariance models using validation that matches the intended application. Refit model choices inside training folds.
  • Use a Gaussian driving process in place of dice to construct a genuinely Gaussian spatial model. Compare its joint distribution with the discrete overlapping-dice process.

Prepare the physical seminar

Groups of three or four need labelled regular dice, four low/high irregular dice for the exchange exercise, controller labels, cups, a tray, graph paper and a shared-contribution board. Seven values for a spatial world can be generated by rolling one die seven times and recording the results on cards. A recorder preserves trial order and links; an observer estimates fuzzy totals; a checker retains clear reference values. Rotate roles, and retain observer identifiers when investigating measurement effects.

Pilot the cup visibility and the time needed for a complete trial. Standardize viewing time, lighting and handling. Moving fixed face values requires careful transfer without shaking. An independent replicate must be reset as specified; a continuing history must preserve its state.

Prepare the data and assessment

The worked R script supplies reproducible splits, real observations and answer calculations. Student handouts receive only training data and target predictors. Keep validation outcomes separate until prediction submission. Real field measurements do not come with a known latent truth, and a toy mechanism cannot establish how a particular ecological system works.

Use the final assessment responses to choose the next topic. Frequent errors about conditional denominators call for another paired-data task; errors about covariance call for more mechanism comparisons; errors about uncertainty call for repeated prediction checks before a longer treatment of Gaussian processes.

Held-back simulation references

Student exports contain observed training data and revealed validation outcomes. Simulated training references are stored separately in this browser. Read them after original forecasts are frozen. Physical experiments require a checker’s separate reference record.
