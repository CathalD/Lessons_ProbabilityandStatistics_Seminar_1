Instructor guide · Three hours

Teach the method.
Then change the dice.

An introduction to probability and statistics for empirical scientists, centred on observation, estimation and predictions tested against new data.

Lesson 1 keeps rolls independent. The regular dice game establishes the method. The irregular dice game changes the outcome distribution while preserving the observational workflow. Linked outcomes, covariance and Gaussian processes belong in Lesson 2.

Set up three dice per experiment

Each group of 3–4 needs three regular six-sided dice, three relabelled six-sided dice, a clear cup, a translucent cup, an opaque cup, a rolling tray and graph paper. Dice can be used sequentially if kits are limited. Label every face of the irregular dice with an integer from 0–9; repeats are permitted, and the three dice can have different labels. Keep face lists out of the student materials.

The regular dice total ranges from 3–18, with 216 equally likely ordered face combinations and 16 possible totals. Three relabelled dice still have 216 ordered face combinations when each face is equally likely, even if labels repeat. Their total can lie within 0–27; the actual support depends on the labels. Relabelling a die does not by itself make its physical faces unequally likely.

Pilot the translucent cup. Keep lighting, distance and viewing time fixed, with enough information for a noisy reading. Use the same dice and rolling procedure for training and validation within each game. Agree in advance how to handle cocked or stacked dice.

Keep the three roles distinct

  • Clear: the reference value. A designated checker records it separately, without giving it to the students during training.
  • Fuzzy: the students’ observed total. Students build their model from these measurements alone.
  • Opaque: a new independent roll. Students commit to their predictions first; only then are true totals revealed for the ten-roll validation set.

For a reference reading, estimate the fuzzy total first and then lift the cover without moving the dice. Only the checker sees and records that truth. Clear and fuzzy readings of the same roll are paired measurements, not independent new outcomes. Each subsequent roll is freshly randomized.

Use paper or a separate instructor notebook for physical clear reference values. The student website does not ask for them during training. If you later show calibration errors, do so after the original predictions and validation results have been recorded.

Give students time to make the model

The mass-function and CDF canvases begin as blank axes. Ask students to draw before opening the computed comparison. Text descriptions provide a keyboard alternative. Function fields are intentionally blank. Print worksheets if students prefer to work on paper, and export the notebook to retain typed work, drawings, measurements and locked forecasts.

For discrete readings, p̂Y(y) = n(y)/N, and F̂Y(x) is the proportion of readings at or below x. For integer endpoints, P̂(a ≤ Y ≤ b) = F̂Y(b) − F̂Y(a − 1). Some texts call a discrete mass function f(x); here p is used for mass and f for continuous density to make the distinction explicit.

Use the first game to follow the whole chain: count a measured outcome, normalize the counts, express the estimate as a function, use the function to solve an interval question, predict a frequency in new trials, then compare. This is a frequentist introduction through relative frequencies and repeated sampling. It is not a claim that all observational science, or all empirical modelling, is exclusively frequentist.

The measurement problem is part of the science

Fuzzy data estimate the distribution of observations Y. They do not automatically identify the distribution of true totals S. A candidate model is Y = S + ε; independence, zero mean and Gaussian error are additional assumptions, not consequences of calling an error “noise”. Without a known or calibrated observation model, multiple true distributions and error mechanisms can explain the same observed distribution.

The empirical interval frequencies can be used as a first predictive model for true totals if students explicitly assume the measurement error is negligible or accept the approximation. The ten true validation outcomes test the consequences. Later reveal held-back calibration errors to distinguish measurement effects from process effects, while emphasizing that ten validation trials are a small sample.

Under independent additive error, Var(Y) = Var(S) + Var(ε). A blurry view need not satisfy this model: guesses can be pulled toward the middle, and their spread can even decrease. Leave covariance calculations to Lesson 2.

Simulation and physical observations

Each experiment offers either physical data entry or simulation. The source choice freezes after data collection begins to avoid silently combining different processes. In simulation, each trial independently selects one of six equally likely faces from each of three fixed dice.

The simulated instrument adds −1 with probability 0.15, 0 with probability 0.70, or +1 with probability 0.15, then clips the reported total to the broad physical limits (3–18 or 0–27). It is a simple synthetic sensor, not a Gaussian error model and not an emulation of human vision. Clipping can introduce bias near a boundary. The page labels simulated readings as synthetic. Clear training truths are not displayed or included in the student notebook export.

Assess predictions on ten fresh rolls

Students select three intervals and submit probabilities from 0–1. Intervals may overlap. If they partition the possible true totals, probabilities must sum to 1. Multiplying q by 10 gives an expected count, which can be fractional; it is not a promise about the realized count.

Locking freezes the training data, interval endpoints, probabilities and original explanation. Validation observations stay separate. Simulated opaque rolls require a roll action followed by a reveal action. Physical totals can be recorded after the roll is revealed. Each game ends after ten outcomes. The website shows predicted and observed counts plus a binomial reference for sampling variation.

That binomial reference treats the estimated q as fixed. It does not account for parameter estimation or measurement uncertainty. Avoid a pass/fail calibration claim from ten trials. Ask whether discrepancy could reflect finite sampling, poor measurement, limited training data or an unstated process change.

Exit questions

  1. What does your p̂Y describe? Under what assumption can it predict the true opaque total?
  2. For integer-valued X, explain the difference between P(2 < X < 4) and F(4) − F(2).
  3. Why did the irregular dice need a new model even though the procedure stayed the same?
  4. If you gather ten times as much training data, what uncertainty might shrink, and what uncertainty about the next independent roll remains?

Expected points: observed versus true variables; F(4) − F(2) includes 4; changed face labels alter the process distribution; more data can improve estimates of the process while fresh-outcome variation persists.
